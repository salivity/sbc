How SBCs Coordinate Multi-Axis Industrial Robots

Single Board Computers (SBCs) serve as the central processing unit in light industrial robotics, translating high-level path commands into synchronized, deterministic motor actions across multiple axes. By leveraging real-time computing environments, kinematics engines, and high-speed industrial communication protocols, an SBC ensures that all robotic joints move in perfect unison to achieve smooth, accurate trajectories.

Kinematics and Trajectory Planning

At the core of multi-axis coordination is the translation of spatial coordinates into individual joint angles. The SBC executes inverse kinematics algorithms to determine the exact position required for each axis at any given moment. Simultaneously, trajectory planning software calculates motion profiles—often using trapezoidal or S-curve acceleration models—to ensure smooth acceleration, minimize mechanical jerk, and synchronize arrival times across all axes so that the robot's end-effector follows the desired path.

Deterministic Real-Time Execution

Standard operating systems introduce unpredictable latency (jitter), which can cause desynchronization in multi-axis setups. To overcome this, SBCs in industrial environments run real-time extensions, such as Linux with the PREEMPT_RT patch, Xenomai, or dedicated Real-Time Operating Systems (RTOS). These real-time kernels guarantee that motion control loops execute at strict, predictable intervals (often between 1 kHz and 4 kHz), ensuring that target positions and velocity setpoints are calculated and dispatched without delay.

Industrial Fieldbus Communication

Rather than generating raw electrical pulses for every motor directly, modern SBCs coordinate multi-axis systems by communicating with intelligent motor drives via deterministic fieldbuses such as EtherCAT, CANopen, or real-time Ethernet. Through these networks, the SBC acts as the master node, broadcasting synchronized position commands to each motor driver in cyclical timeframes. Distributed clock mechanisms within protocols like EtherCAT synchronize internal drive timers to sub-microsecond precision, guaranteeing that all motors execute their commands simultaneously.

Direct Signal Generation and Co-Processing

In architectures where lower-cost, non-networked stepper or servo drives are used, the SBC offloads high-speed pulse generation to dedicated hardware modules. This can include onboard Programmable Real-Time Units (PRUs), secondary microcontroller units (MCUs), or FPGA companion chips. The main SBC processor computes the complex kinematics and feeds trajectory segments to these co-processors, which then generate the high-frequency step and direction signals required by the motor drivers.

Closed-Loop Feedback Integration

To maintain positional accuracy and detect collisions, the SBC continuously monitors feedback from encoders, torque sensors, and limit switches. The SBC compares actual joint positions against commanded trajectories in real time, executing proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control loops to correct for tracking errors, mechanical load variations, and thermal drift, thereby ensuring reliable and repeatable industrial performance.
