How Hardware RTCs Maintain Time During SBC Power Cycles

Single board computers (SBCs) typically lack onboard battery-backed clocks, causing them to lose track of time whenever main power is disconnected. An external hardware real-time clock (RTC) maintains accurate timekeeping through power cycles by utilizing an independent backup power source, a dedicated low-power oscillator, an automatic power-switching circuit, and standard bus communication. When main system power is removed, the RTC switches to auxiliary battery power and continues counting seconds in hardware. Upon reboot, the SBC's operating system reads the stored timestamp over a digital interface, immediately restoring the correct system time without relying on an internet connection or Network Time Protocol (NTP) servers.

Dedicated Backup Power Sources

An external RTC module features a dedicated, independent power source, typically a 3V lithium coin cell (such as a CR2032) or a high-capacity supercapacitor. Because RTC integrated circuits (ICs) consume minuscule amounts of current—often in the range of 200 to 500 nanoamperes while in standby—a standard coin cell can power the timekeeping circuitry continuously for several years without external intervention.

Automatic Power-Switching Circuitry

RTC chips (such as the DS3231 or PCF8523) contain internal voltage-sensing and power-routing circuitry. Under normal operation, the module draws power from the SBC's primary 3.3V or 5V rail:

  1. Power Drop Detection: As the SBC shuts down or loses power, the primary supply voltage (\(V_{CC}\)) drops below a predefined threshold or falls below the backup battery voltage (\(V_{BAT}\)).
  2. Seamless Transition: The internal power-switch circuit automatically isolates the primary power rail and engages the backup battery. This switch occurs instantly with zero interruption to the internal counting registers.
  3. Power Restoration: When main power returns to the SBC, the circuit senses that \(V_{CC}\) has risen above \(V_{BAT}\) and shifts the load back to the primary supply rail, saving battery capacity.

Low-Power Crystal Oscillators

To track time with high precision, the RTC relies on a 32.768 kHz quartz crystal oscillator. This specific frequency is used because \(32,768\) is \(2^{15}\), allowing simple digital binary frequency dividers to reduce the oscillation to an exact 1 Hz pulse (one count per second).

Higher-accuracy RTC modules integrate a Temperature-Compensated Crystal Oscillator (TCXO). The TCXO contains an internal temperature sensor that adjusts the oscillator's capacitance to compensate for thermal drift. This ensures the clock maintains synchronization within a few seconds per year, regardless of ambient temperature changes while the SBC is turned off.

Time Synchronization at Boot

When the single board computer powers back on, synchronization between the external RTC and the operating system occurs in a multi-step sequence:

  1. Bus Initialization: The SBC's processor boots and initializes hardware communication buses, most commonly I2C or SPI.
  2. Kernel Driver Execution: The operating system kernel loads the corresponding RTC device driver (e.g., rtc-ds1307 or rtc-ds3231).
  3. Hardware Clock Read: Early in the boot process, initialization systems (such as systemd or standard init scripts) execute a hardware clock query command (equivalent to hwclock --hctosys).
  4. System Clock Update: The operating system reads the registers containing current seconds, minutes, hours, days, months, and years from the RTC, initializing the software system clock before user applications or network interfaces load.
