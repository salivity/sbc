Power Delivery via PoE on Single Board Computers

Power over Ethernet (PoE) allows a single board computer (SBC) to receive both high-speed data and electrical power through a single standard Ethernet cable (Cat5e or better). This eliminates the need for a separate DC power supply or USB cable, simplifying remote deployments such as IoT nodes, smart cameras, and edge computing clusters. Power delivery is strictly regulated through standardized hardware protocols, step-down conversion stages, and onboard power management circuits to ensure the SBC receives a stable, safe operating voltage.

The PoE Power Delivery Chain

Power delivery involves two main physical endpoints: the Power Sourcing Equipment (PSE), such as a PoE-capable network switch or midspan power injector, and the Powered Device (PD), which is the SBC or an attached PoE mezzanine board (such as a PoE HAT).

  1. Power Sourcing Equipment (PSE): Injects direct current (DC) into unused twisted pairs (Alternative B) or alongside the data signals via phantom power (Alternative A) at a nominal voltage between 44V and 57V DC.
  2. Ethernet Transformer & Rectifier: The SBC's RJ45 magnetic jack routes the incoming high-voltage DC to bridge rectifiers, ensuring proper polarity regardless of cable wiring.
  3. PD Controller: A dedicated controller chip communicates with the PSE to negotiate power availability and monitor line status.
  4. DC-DC Step-Down Converter: A switch-mode buck converter steps down the high-voltage PoE input (typically ~48V) to the SBC’s primary internal operating voltage (usually 5V DC).

Power Negotiation Process

Before high voltage is applied across the Ethernet cable, the PSE and SBC execute a handshake sequence dictated by IEEE standards (such as IEEE 802.3af for up to 15.4W or IEEE 802.3at for up to 30W):

  • Detection: The PSE transmits low-voltage pulses (2.7V to 10.1V) to detect a standard 25kΩ signature resistance on the SBC’s PD controller. This prevents the PSE from sending high voltage to non-PoE network devices.
  • Classification: Once detected, the PSE measures the current drawn by the PD to determine its power class (Class 0 through Class 4), identifying how much power the SBC requires under maximum load.
  • Power-Up: Upon successful classification, the PSE ramps up the voltage to the full ~48V level.
  • Continuous Monitoring: The PSE constantly monitors current draw. If the SBC is unplugged or an overcurrent event occurs, the PSE cuts power within milliseconds to protect the hardware.

Voltage Regulation and Board Integration

Because the core processors, RAM, and peripheral interfaces on an SBC operate at low DC voltages (typically 5V, 3.3V, 1.8V, and below), efficient step-down conversion is critical:

  • High-Efficiency Buck Conversion: The onboard or add-on DC-DC converter steps the 48V input down to a clean 5V rail with efficiency ratings typically between 85% and 92%. High efficiency minimizes heat generation inside compact enclosures.
  • Galvanic Isolation: In standard-compliant designs, a flyback or forward transformer magnetically isolates the high-voltage Ethernet lines from the low-voltage logic circuits. This protects the SBC from ground loops, voltage spikes, and electrostatic discharge (ESD).
  • Power Distribution Network (PDN): The stepped-down 5V supply is routed directly to the SBC's primary power management integrated circuit (PMIC). The PMIC then generates the lower, tightly regulated voltage rails required for the CPU, GPU, memory, and auxiliary inputs like USB ports and GPIO pins.
