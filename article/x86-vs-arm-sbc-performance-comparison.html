x86 vs ARM SBC: Performance Comparison

Single-board computers (SBCs) built on x86 and ARM architectures offer fundamentally different performance profiles tailored to distinct computing demands. While ARM-based SBCs dominate in energy efficiency and low-power processing, x86-based models typically excel in raw single-core compute power, complex multitasking, and native software execution. Choosing between them depends on whether your workload prioritizes maximum processing capability and broad software compatibility or power efficiency and hardware-specific acceleration.

Raw Compute and Processing Power

x86 SBCs, powered by processors such as Intel Celeron, Intel N-series, or AMD Ryzen Embedded, generally outperform standard ARM SBCs in raw CPU benchmarks. Their microarchitecture favors higher instructions per cycle (IPC) and higher sustained boost frequencies. While modern flagship ARM SBCs equipped with Cortex-A76 or Cortex-A78 cores narrow this performance gap, x86 processors still hold the advantage in heavy mathematical computations, complex compiling tasks, and single-threaded operations.

Graphics and Multimedia Performance

Graphics capabilities vary significantly between the two architectures:

  • x86 SBCs: Integrated GPUs from Intel (UHD, Iris Xe) and AMD (Radeon) provide mature driver support across Windows and Linux, delivering consistent 3D rendering, OpenGL, and Vulkan performance suitable for light gaming and desktop graphical user interfaces.
  • ARM SBCs: Often integrate GPUs like ARM Mali or proprietary cores that feature dedicated hardware engines for video decoding (such as H.264, H.265, and AV1) and Neural Processing Units (NPUs). These allow ARM boards to handle 4K or 8K media playback and edge machine learning tasks with minimal CPU overhead.

Memory Bandwidth and I/O Throughput

Input/output (I/O) architecture strongly impacts real-world performance. x86 boards routinely feature native PCIe lanes that support high-speed NVMe solid-state drives, USB 3.2 Gen 2, dual gigabit or 2.5GbE LAN, and SATA interfaces without bus bottlenecks. ARM boards have traditionally relied on slower eMMC storage or shared bus interfaces, though newer generations increasingly feature dedicated PCIe lanes for M.2 SSDs. In sustained data-transfer scenarios, such as network-attached storage (NAS) or database hosting, x86 boards usually provide superior sustained throughput.

Power Efficiency and Thermal Throttling

ARM processors utilize a RISC (Reduced Instruction Set Computer) design, allowing them to deliver exceptional performance-per-watt. An ARM SBC typically consumes between 2W and 15W under full load and can operate reliably with passive cooling. Conversely, x86 SBCs draw significantly more power, often ranging from 6W to 25W or higher under load. Without active cooling (such as heatsinks paired with fans), x86 SBCs are more susceptible to thermal throttling, which temporarily reduces clock speeds to prevent overheating during sustained high-load operations.

Software Optimization and Architecture Overhead

Performance is heavily influenced by how software interacts with the hardware:

  • x86 Platforms: Native compatibility with standard x86-64 binaries means operating systems like Windows 10/11, Ubuntu, and specialized hypervisors run with full driver support and zero emulation penalty.
  • ARM Platforms: Highly efficient when running software natively compiled for ARM64 (aarch64). However, running legacy x86 software on an ARM platform requires dynamic translation or emulation (e.g., Box64, QEMU), which introduces a substantial performance hit.
