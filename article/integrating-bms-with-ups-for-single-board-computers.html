Integrating BMS with UPS for Single Board Computers

Integrating a Battery Management System (BMS) with an Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS) for a Single Board Computer (SBC) creates a dependable power solution that prevents unexpected shutdowns, data corruption, and battery damage. This integration combines safety circuitry, power routing, and smart communication to deliver continuous power during outages and initiate graceful operating system shutdowns when battery reserves run low.

The Core Division of Roles

A reliable SBC backup power setup divides responsibilities between two specialized functional blocks:

  • The Battery Management System (BMS): Operates at the cell level. It monitors cell voltages, balances multi-cell configurations, and protects against overcharging, over-discharging, short circuits, and extreme temperatures.
  • The Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS): Operates at the system level. It accepts external utility power, charges the battery via the BMS, provides a steady output voltage (typically 5V via USB-C or GPIO pins) to the SBC, and manages instantaneous power source switching.

In many compact SBC designs, the BMS and UPS electronics reside on a single expansion board (often called a HAT or shield), while in custom or industrial setups, they exist as discrete modules connected in series.

Hardware and Power Path Integration

The physical power delivery path connects the external power source, the battery assembly, and the SBC load:

  1. Charging Circuit to BMS: External input power feeds a battery charger IC located on the UPS board. This charger outputs regulated voltage and current to the BMS, which directs the energy into the lithium-ion or LiFePO4 cells while maintaining cell balance.
  2. Dynamic Power Path Management: Modern UPS systems use power-path MOSFETs or ideal diode controllers. When mains power is present, the input directly powers the SBC while simultaneously routing excess current to the charger. If the main input drops, the controller switches to the battery supply within microseconds—fast enough that the SBC's input capacitors do not discharge below the operational threshold.
  3. Output Voltage Regulation: Lithium battery voltages vary as they discharge (e.g., 4.2V down to 3.0V for standard Li-ion). A synchronous boost or buck-boost converter on the UPS steps this varying voltage up to a stable 5.1V or 12V required by the SBC.

Data and Telemetry Integration

For intelligent operation, the BMS/UPS setup must communicate its state to the SBC operating system:

  • Fuel Gauge ICs: Specialized ICs (such as the MAX1704x or BQ27441) track battery impedance, voltage, current flow (coulomb counting), and State of Charge (SoC).
  • Communication Bus: The fuel gauge interfaces with the SBC over I2C, SMBus, or UART lines.
  • Status Signals: Dedicated GPIO pins transmit critical discrete signals, such as AC Power Loss, Battery Low, and Power-Cut acknowledgment.

Software Integration and Graceful Shutdown

Hardware continuity is only half the solution; the SBC software must react intelligently to battery states:

  1. Background Monitoring Daemon: A lightweight service (such as a custom Python script, shell daemon, or Network UPS Tools) periodically polls the fuel gauge over I2C.
  2. Mains Loss Event: When mains power disconnects, the UPS signals the SBC. The SBC can log the event or reduce CPU frequency to extend battery life.
  3. Low Capacity Threshold: If the BMS reports that the battery capacity has fallen below a safe limit (e.g., 10% to 15%), the monitoring script issues a safe shutdown command (sudo poweroff or shutdown -h now).
  4. Final Power Cutoff: After the SBC finishes unmounting filesystems and halts, the UPS detects the drop in power draw or waits out a predefined hardware timer before cutting output power entirely. This prevents the BMS from entering a deep-discharge lockout state.
