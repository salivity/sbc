Using Single Board Computers for LEO Satellite Tracking

Single-board computers (SBCs) offer an affordable, energy-efficient platform for establishing automated ground stations that track, receive, and decode signals from low-Earth-orbit (LEO) amateur satellites. By interfacing with Software Defined Radios (SDRs) and running specialized orbital calculation algorithms, SBCs handle the entire reception pipeline from scheduled pass prediction to payload data demodulation.

Core Hardware Architecture

An SBC-based ground station relies on the single-board computer acting as the central processing and control unit. A typical setup comprises:

  • Single-Board Computer: Units such as a Raspberry Pi or similar ARM-based boards provide the general-purpose computing needed to manage schedules, process digital signals, and control external hardware.
  • Software Defined Radio (SDR): Connected via USB, low-cost SDR dongles (such as RTL-SDR, Airspy, or HackRF) digitize raw radio frequency (RF) signals received by the antenna.
  • Antenna System: Stationary omnidirectional antennas (like Turnstile or QFH antennas) are common for broad coverage, while directional Yagi antennas mounted on azimuth-elevation rotators can be driven by the SBC's GPIO pins for higher-gain tracking.
  • Filtering and Amplification: Low Noise Amplifiers (LNAs) and bandpass filters placed near the antenna improve signal-to-noise ratio before the signal enters the SDR.

Orbital Tracking and Pass Prediction

LEO satellites orbit the Earth at altitudes between 160 and 2,000 kilometers, moving rapidly and only remaining visible to a ground station for roughly 5 to 15 minutes per pass.

To track these satellites, the SBC runs prediction software such as Gpredict, Predict, or the SatNOGS client. These programs ingest Two-Line Element (TLE) data sets periodically downloaded via the internet. Using the SGP4 (Simplified General Perturbations-4) orbital model, the SBC calculates the exact rise time, trajectory, azimuth, elevation, and maximum altitude of upcoming satellite passes relative to the station's geographic coordinates.

Doppler Shift Management

Due to the high velocity of LEO satellites relative to a fixed observer, the carrier frequency undergoes a significant Doppler shift during a pass. As the satellite approaches, the observed frequency is higher than the nominal transmission frequency; as it moves away, the frequency drops.

The SBC manages this in real time. It calculates the instantaneous relative velocity and adjusts the tuning frequency of the SDR continuously throughout the pass. Alternatively, wideband baseband I/Q data is recorded, and the Doppler correction is applied digitally in post-processing via software routines.

Signal Processing and Data Demodulation

Once a pass begins, the SBC executes automated capture scripts. The process flows through several digital signal processing (DSP) stages:

  1. RF Capture: The SDR streams raw I/Q samples to the SBC over USB.
  2. Filtering and Decimation: The SBC reduces the sample rate to isolate the target signal bandwidth, conserving CPU and memory resources.
  3. Demodulation: Software decoders (such as GNU Radio, SatDump, Direwolf, or multimon-ng) process the audio or baseband stream to recover the underlying data.
  4. Payload Extraction: The station decodes specific telemetry, such as Automatic Packet Reporting System (APRS) packets, weather satellite imagery (e.g., NOAA APT or Meteor-M LRPT), or scientific telemetry from CubeSats.

Automated Network Integration

Modern SBC ground stations often operate headlessly as part of distributed networks, such as the open-source SatNOGS network. In this configuration, the SBC automatically queries a central network database for observation requests, executes observations without human intervention, and uploads the recorded audio, waterfalls, and decoded telemetry back to a centralized public repository for community analysis.
