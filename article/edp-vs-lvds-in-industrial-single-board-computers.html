eDP vs LVDS in Industrial Single Board Computers

This article explores the primary differences between Embedded DisplayPort (eDP) and Low-Voltage Differential Signaling (LVDS) video interfaces used on industrial single-board computers (SBCs). It covers critical technical distinctions, including data bandwidth, resolution capabilities, cabling design, power efficiency, and long-term processor compatibility, helping embedded system designers select the optimal display interface for their industrial applications.

Understanding LVDS and eDP

  • LVDS (Low-Voltage Differential Signaling): Introduced in the 1990s, LVDS has long been the legacy standard for internal display connections in industrial environments. It transmits data using differential voltage swings across multiple copper wire pairs.
  • eDP (Embedded DisplayPort): Introduced by VESA as a standardized internal interface based on the DisplayPort protocol, eDP is the modern successor to LVDS, designed specifically for high-bandwidth embedded display connections.

Key Differences Between eDP and LVDS

1. Bandwidth and Maximum Resolution

  • LVDS: Typically limited to single-channel (up to 1366×768 or 1280×800) or dual-channel (up to 1920×1080 at 60Hz) configurations. Supporting higher resolutions requires quad-channel setups, which dramatically increases pin counts and design complexity.
  • eDP: Provides significantly higher data transfer rates (up to 8.1 Gbps per lane in eDP 1.4+). A single 4-lane eDP interface easily drives 4K (3840×2160) displays at high refresh rates, with newer standards supporting up to 8K resolutions.

2. Wiring and Cable Complexity

  • LVDS: Requires a high wire count. A standard dual-channel LVDS connection needs between 16 and 20 wires (including clock pairs, ground, and power), leading to thicker, stiffer, and more expensive cable harnesses.
  • eDP: Uses fewer physical traces. An eDP interface requires only 1 to 4 high-speed data lanes, one bidirectional AUX channel for sideband communication/control, and a hot-plug detect (HPD) line. This drastically reduces connector size and enables thinner, more flexible micro-coaxial cabling.

3. Native Processor Support and Bridge Chips

  • LVDS: Modern x86 (Intel Core/Atom, AMD) and advanced ARM processors have phased out native LVDS controllers. Driving an LVDS panel from a modern SBC usually requires an onboard active converter (e.g., eDP-to-LVDS or DSI-to-LVDS bridge chip), adding cost, latency, and component failure points.
  • eDP: Supported natively by virtually all modern industrial processors and system-on-chips (SoCs), simplifying board design, reducing bill-of-materials (BOM) costs, and improving overall system reliability.

4. Power Efficiency and Advanced Features

  • LVDS: Lacks integrated power-management features. Display timing, backlight control, and power sequencing must be handled via separate external signals (PWM, GPIOs).
  • eDP: Incorporates advanced power-saving mechanisms such as Panel Self Refresh (PSR), which allows the GPU to power down during static display states. Control signals (brightness, backlight power, and EDID data) are managed directly over the AUX channel, eliminating dedicated discrete control lines.

5. Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)

  • LVDS: Operates with balanced differential signals that resist external noise, but higher-frequency operation over long, high-wire-count cables can still introduce EMI issues.
  • eDP: Uses lower voltage swings and built-in spread-spectrum clocking (SSC), which reduces peak electromagnetic emissions and allows for easier EMC certification in sensitive industrial settings.

Choosing the Right Interface for Industrial SBCs

Choose LVDS if you are maintaining or retrofitting legacy equipment with existing display enclosures, or when long-term panel supply contracts already dictate an LVDS panel.

Choose eDP for all new industrial SBC designs requiring high resolutions, compact internal routing, lower power consumption, and seamless compatibility with modern processing architectures.
