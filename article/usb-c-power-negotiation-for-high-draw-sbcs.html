USB-C Power Negotiation for High-Draw SBCs

Designing a modern, high-draw single-board computer (SBC) requires moving beyond basic 5V power rails to support dynamic USB Power Delivery (USB-PD). This article outlines the essential hardware design practices needed to ensure reliable power negotiation, safe power distribution, and robust protection against transient faults on high-performance SBCs requiring more than standard USB power levels.

1. Implement Dedicated CC Pull-Down Resistors

For basic 5V/3A (Type-C Current) capability, ensure the Configuration Channel lines (CC1 and CC2) each feature an independent 5.1 kΩ pull-down resistor to ground. A common design failure on early SBCs was tying both CC pins together to a single 5.1 kΩ resistor. This prevents electronically marked (e-marked) cables from establishing a valid connection and will cause smart chargers to withhold power completely.

2. Integrate a Standalone USB-PD Controller

High-draw SBCs requiring 15W to 100W+ (such as 9V, 12V, 15V, or 20V profiles) cannot rely on simple analog pull-down resistors. Designers should implement a dedicated, autonomous USB-PD controller IC (such as solutions from Texas Instruments, STMicroelectronics, or Richtek).

  • Autonomous Operation: The PD controller should negotiate the default power contract before the main system processor boots up.
  • Firmware & I2C Control: Route I2C or SPI lines from the PD controller to the main processor to allow dynamic renegotiation or power telemetry during runtime.

3. Manage Inrush Current and Soft-Start

High-power SBCs often have large input capacitance banks on their primary power stages. When a 20V PD contract is successfully negotiated, instantaneous switching can cause extreme inrush currents, leading to contact erosion, voltage dips on the source, or power cycling.

  • Place back-to-back N-channel MOSFETs on the VBUS entry path as a load switch.
  • Implement controlled gate-drive slew rates to achieve a soft-start ramp (typically 1–10 ms).
  • Keep input bulk capacitance within the USB-PD specifications (a maximum of 10 µF directly on the connector side before the isolation switch).

4. Provide Robust Overvoltage and ESD Protection

Dynamic voltage negotiation exposes components to varying levels of VBUS (from 5V up to 20V, or 48V under USB-PD Extended Power Range).

  • Overvoltage Protection (OVP): Implement a dedicated OVP switch that disconnects downstream circuitry within nanoseconds if VBUS exceeds the negotiated threshold.
  • ESD and Short-to-VBUS Protection: CC and SBU (Sideband Use) pins run directly adjacent to the high-voltage VBUS pins in the USB-C receptacle. Use low-capacitance TVS diode arrays on CC lines that are rated for short-to-VBUS conditions (rated for at least 24V tolerant continuous DC).

5. Efficient Step-Down (Buck) Conversion

Negotiating higher voltages (e.g., 20V at 3A–5A) reduces I²R cable losses compared to high-current 5V rails, but it places the burden of thermal and power conversion on the SBC.

  • Use high-efficiency synchronous buck converters to step down the high VBUS voltage to internal 5V, 3.3V, and core power rails.
  • Ensure low ESR ceramic capacitors and properly rated inductors are used to handle ripple currents without excessive thermal dissipation near sensitive processing units.

6. Implement Fallback and Low-Power Boot Logic

Not all users will connect an adequate high-wattage power supply. The SBC power management architecture should include fallback logic:

  • If only standard 5V/3A or 5V/1.5A is negotiated, the SBC should restrict its clock frequencies, disable high-draw peripherals (like USB 3.0 ports, PCIe devices, or auxiliary headers), or prompt the user via an LED indicator rather than brown-out during boot.
