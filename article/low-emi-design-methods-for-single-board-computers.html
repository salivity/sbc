Low EMI Design Methods for Single Board Computers

Single board computers (SBCs) operate at high clock frequencies with densely packed components, making them prone to emitting electromagnetic interference (EMI). To meet stringent electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) regulations, hardware engineers implement specific design methodologies throughout the PCB layout, schematic design, and enclosure integration phases. This article outlines the primary engineering strategies—including stackup planning, high-speed signal routing, power distribution network optimization, clock management, and physical shielding—that allow modern SBCs to achieve exceptionally low electromagnetic radiation profiles.

Strategic PCB Layer Stackup and Grounding

The foundation of low-radiation SBC design is an optimized multilayer PCB stackup. Designers typically use a minimum of four to eight layers to isolate noisy signals and provide continuous reference planes.

  • Continuous Ground Planes: Placing solid ground planes directly adjacent to high-speed signal layers creates a low-impedance return path. This minimizes the current loop area, which directly reduces radiated emissions proportional to loop size.
  • Stripline Routing: High-speed traces (such as DDR memory buses and PCIe lanes) are routed on internal layers sandwiched between ground or power planes (stripline) rather than on external layers (microstrip). The outer copper planes act as natural shields against radiation.
  • The 20-H Rule: Power planes are pulled back from the edge of the board relative to the ground plane by a factor of 20 times the dielectric thickness to contain fringing fields at PCB boundaries.

High-Speed Signal Integrity and Trace Geometries

Careful routing prevents signals from acting as inadvertent radiating antennas:

  • Controlled Impedance: Traces are designed with specific characteristic impedance (e.g., 50Ω single-ended, 90Ω or 100Ω differential) to eliminate impedance mismatches that cause signal reflections, ringing, and harmonic emissions.
  • Via Minimization and Stitching: Transitioning high-speed traces between layers creates discontinuities. When layer changes are necessary, ground stitching vias are placed adjacent to signal vias to maintain an uninterrupted return path.
  • Differential Signaling: Protocols like USB, Ethernet, and HDMI utilize tightly coupled differential pairs. Because the currents flow in equal and opposite directions, their magnetic fields cancel out, drastically reducing radiated noise.

Power Distribution Network (PDN) and Decoupling

Fluctuations on the power rail can radiate noise across the entire board. An effective PDN stabilizes voltage and suppresses high-frequency transients:

  • Localized Decoupling Capacitors: Small ceramic capacitors (0.1 µF, 0.01 µF) with low equivalent series inductance (ESL) are placed as close as possible to the power pins of SoCs, processors, and PMICs to provide immediate transient current.
  • Ferrite Beads and Filtering: Ferrite beads and LC filters are placed on power rails entering sensitive analog sections or leaving the board through external connectors to block high-frequency noise propagation.

Clock Management and Slew Rate Control

Clock signals and sharp digital transitions are the most significant contributors to high-frequency EMI spikes:

  • Spread Spectrum Clocking (SSC): SSC deliberately modulates clock frequencies over a narrow range (e.g., ±0.5% to ±1.0%). This distributes spectral energy over a wider bandwidth, lowering the peak radiated amplitude at specific harmonic frequencies.
  • Slew Rate Limiting and Series Termination: Series damping resistors placed near the driver output slow down the rise and fall times of digital signals. Softening these edges removes high-order harmonics without compromising data timing.

I/O Filtering and Physical Shielding

Interfacing cables often act as antennas that radiate internal system noise into the environment:

  • Common-Mode Chokes (CMCs): Installed on high-speed data lines (e.g., USB, Ethernet) near the I/O connectors, CMCs attenuate common-mode noise while allowing differential data to pass unhindered.
  • Board-Level Shielding (BLS): Metal RF shielding cans are soldered directly over the central processor, PMIC, and wireless modules to contain near-field radiated emissions at the source.
  • Chassis Grounding: Connector shells are tied to a dedicated chassis ground, shunting external ESD and internal EMI away from the main digital ground system.
