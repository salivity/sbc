PC/104 Architecture vs Modern Single Board Computers

This article explores the fundamental design differences between legacy, stackable PC/104 architectures and modern single board computers (SBCs). While both solutions cater to embedded systems, industrial computing, and rugged environments, they diverge significantly in their mechanical layouts, interconnect topologies, modularity, and overall approach to Size, Weight, Power, and Cost (SWaP-C).

Mechanical Form Factor and Stacking Architecture

The defining characteristic of PC/104 is its stackable, modular architecture. Measuring roughly 3.6 by 3.8 inches (90 x 96 mm), PC/104 modules feature four-corner mounting holes and self-stacking pin-and-socket bus connectors. This design eliminates the need for a backplane or card cage; peripheral modules (such as I/O cards, power supplies, and communication controllers) stack directly on top of or below the central processor board.

In contrast, modern SBCs typically adopt a monolithic, single-PCB design (such as Pico-ITX, 3.5-inch boards, or proprietary form factors) or rely on a Computer-on-Module (COM/SOM) plus carrier board approach. Rather than stacking multiple independent boards vertically, modern SBC designs integrate the CPU, memory, power regulation, and standard I/O directly onto a single plane, expanding horizontally or via compact mezzanine standards like M.2.

Bus Interfaces and Interconnects

Interconnect technology represents a major evolutionary gap between the two approaches:

  • PC/104 Generations: The original PC/104 standard adapted the desktop ISA bus into a 104-pin rugged header. Subsequent revisions introduced PCI (PC/104-Plus), PCI-X (PCI-104), and high-speed serial lanes like PCIe (PCIe/104 and OneBank). However, routing high-speed differential signals through stack-through pin headers poses signal integrity challenges at modern multi-gigahertz speeds.
  • Modern SBCs: Modern SBCs utilize advanced high-density interconnects, edge connectors, and onboard traces to route multi-lane PCIe Gen 3/4/5, USB 3.x/USB4, MIPI, and high-speed Ethernet directly between the processor, SoC, and peripheral controllers. This direct-routing approach minimizes impedance mismatches and latency.

Ruggedness and Environmental Tolerance

PC/104 was engineered specifically for extreme shock, vibration, and harsh industrial or military operating conditions. The physical stack forms a rigid, multi-point fastened cube that withstands intense mechanical stresses without card dislocation.

Modern SBCs achieve high reliability through component integration and surface-mount technology rather than mechanical stacking. By eliminating stack-through connectors and reducing the overall number of mechanical interconnects, modern SBCs minimize potential failure points. Ruggedness is typically addressed through conduction cooling plates, conformal coatings, and rugged enclosures rather than heavy mechanical standoff structures.

Size, Weight, Power, and Cost (SWaP-C)

Modern SBCs provide superior compute density compared to PC/104 systems:

  • Integration Density: Highly integrated System-on-Chips (SoCs) allow modern SBCs to fit complete compute platforms—including multi-core x86 or ARM processors, GPUs, memory, and networking—onto a board smaller than a single PC/104 module.
  • Vertical Profile: A fully functional PC/104 system often requires 3 to 6 stacked layers, resulting in significant vertical bulk and weight. An equivalent modern SBC accomplishes the same functionality within a low-profile, single-board envelope.
  • Power and Thermal Management: PC/104 stacks create trapped thermal zones between layers, making heat dissipation difficult without complex conduction frames. Modern SBCs generally feature open layouts that allow direct mounting of single heat sinks, cold plates, or heat pipes.

Modularity and Upgradeability

PC/104 offers true physical modularity: developers can swap a CPU module while retaining all specialized I/O modules in the stack, or add new I/O boards as requirements change.

Modern SBCs sacrifice some layer-by-layer modularity for integration, cost efficiency, and speed. However, they compensate through standard miniature expansion slots (such as M.2 for storage, AI acceleration, or wireless modems) or by using COM Express / SMARC modules on custom carrier boards, decoupling the processing unit from the application-specific I/O.
