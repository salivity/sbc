Designing Custom Daughterboards for Single Board Computers

Designing custom daughterboards—often called hats, capes, or shields—expands the functionality of a Single Board Computer (SBC) for specialized applications. Successfully integrating a secondary board requires a balanced approach to both physical compatibility and circuit design. This guide covers the essential mechanical and electrical considerations required to design a reliable, high-performance daughterboard for any SBC.

Mechanical Considerations

Mechanical design determines whether the daughterboard will physically fit, remain securely attached, and operate within the thermal boundaries of the host system.

1. Form Factor and Keepout Zones

  • Board Dimensions and Alignment: Match the outer dimensions, corner radiuses, and mounting hole patterns of the host SBC. Standardizing on the host's reference mechanical drawings prevents misalignments.
  • Component Height Clearances (Z-Height): SBCs feature tall components such as Ethernet jacks, USB ports, SoC heatsinks, and electrolytic capacitors. Establish a defined keepout zone on the bottom of the daughterboard to prevent components from colliding with the SBC's top-side parts.
  • Cutouts and Port Access: Provide cutouts for internal connectors (e.g., MIPI-CSI, MIPI-DSI, ribbon cables) or antennas located on the host board that need to pass through the daughterboard.

2. Header and Connector Selection

  • Pin Pitch and Stacking Height: Use appropriate female or pass-through headers that match the pitch (typically 2.54mm or 2.0mm) of the SBC's expansion header. Select header pin lengths that maintain sufficient clearance between the two PCBs.
  • Connector Alignment: High-pin-count connectors require tight manufacturing tolerances. Misaligned pins can cause mechanical strain, bent headers, or intermittent contact.
  • Keying and Shrouding: Whenever possible, use keyed connectors or asymmetrical pin arrangements to prevent inverted or offset mating.

3. Structural Support and Fasteners

  • Standoffs: Relying solely on the expansion header for mechanical retention causes trace fatigue and connector damage over time. Use threaded standoffs (e.g., M2.5 or M3) at all designated mounting points.
  • Vibration and Shock Resistance: In mobile or industrial environments, lock washers, thread-locking fluid, or friction-fit multi-point connectors prevent the boards from separating under mechanical stress.

4. Thermal Management and Airflow

  • Heat Dissipation Path: Avoid placing heat-sensitive components directly above the SBC's primary processor or Power Management IC (PMIC).
  • Airflow Obstruction: Stacking a daughterboard directly over the main processor creates an insulating air pocket. Integrate cutouts, ventilation holes, or dedicated thermal vias to allow passive convection or clearance for an active cooling fan.

Electrical Considerations

Electrical design ensures reliable power delivery, cross-board communication, and protection against electrical damage to both the daughterboard and the SBC.

1. Power Architecture and Current Budgets

  • Host Rail Limitations: SBC power rails (usually 5V and 3.3V) have strict current limits. Calculate the total current draw of the daughterboard. If the current requirements exceed the SBC’s supply capacity, implement a dedicated power input on the daughterboard.
  • Back-Powering Prevention: If the daughterboard has an independent power supply, ensure it does not inadvertently back-feed power into the SBC’s rails via pull-up resistors or I/O pins, which can cause erratic boot behavior or hardware damage. Use ideal diodes or MOSFET-based power-path management.
  • Decoupling and Power Cleanliness: Place decoupling capacitors close to all active ICs. Switching regulators on the daughterboard should have careful inductor placement and ground routing to prevent noise from coupling into the SBC's power planes.

2. Logic Levels and Signal Integrity

  • Voltage Level Matching: Most modern SBCs operate on 3.3V or 1.8V logic levels and are generally not 5V-tolerant. Incorporate bidirectional logic level shifters if interfacing with legacy 5V devices.
  • High-Speed Bus Routing: For high-speed interfaces like SPI, USB, PCIe, or SDIO, maintain controlled impedance (e.g., 50-ohm single-ended, 90/100-ohm differential), match trace lengths, and route over a continuous ground reference plane.
  • Ground Returns: Ensure low-impedance ground returns through the header pins. Inadequate grounding between the host and daughterboard leads to ground bounce, EMI issues, and communication errors.

3. Pin Multiplexing and Boot Strapping

  • Multi-Function GPIOs: Check the SBC documentation for pin multiplexing constraints (pinmux). Ensure selected GPIOs do not conflict with alternate internal functions needed by the host operating system.
  • Boot Strapping Pins: Certain processor pins dictate boot modes (e.g., eMMC vs. SD card) when high or low during power-up. Avoid using boot configuration pins on the daughterboard unless the default pull-up/pull-down states are strictly preserved.
  • Default Power-On States: Determine whether host GPIOs float, pull high, or pull low during the bootloader phase to prevent accidental activation of actuators, relays, or power stages.

4. Protection and EMC

  • Electrostatic Discharge (ESD): Add TVS diodes to any external-facing user interfaces (USB, push buttons, terminal blocks, sensor lines) to clamp voltage spikes before they propagate to the SBC.
  • Over-Voltage and Reverse-Polarity Protection: Protect auxiliary power inputs using fuses (PTC or one-time), Zener diodes, or reverse-polarity protection circuits.
  • Electromagnetic Compatibility (EMC): Keep high-frequency switching loops small, utilize multi-layer boards with dedicated ground and power planes, and route sensitive analog signals far from high-speed digital buses.
