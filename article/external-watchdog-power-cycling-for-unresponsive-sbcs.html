External Watchdog Power Cycling for Unresponsive SBCs

An external watchdog circuit forcefully power cycles an unresponsive single-board computer (SBC) by continuously monitoring a dedicated "heartbeat" signal from the SBC and physically cutting input power if that signal ceases. Unlike internal software watchdogs that can hang along with the main processor, an external hardware circuit operates on an independent power rail and uses a high-side switch to execute a true cold reboot. This article explains the underlying mechanism, core components, and step-by-step process required to implement hardware-level power cycling for locked-up SBCs.

The Mechanism of Hardware Power Cycling

Internal watchdog timers often share the same system clocks, power rails, or silicon dies as the main processor. During severe kernel panics, bus lockups, or latch-up states, internal watchdogs can fail entirely. An external watchdog circuit resolves this by completely severing the SBC's DC supply rail, discharging residual energy, and reapplying power to force a clean hardware boot.

Essential Circuit Components

  1. Watchdog Timer / Supervisor IC: A low-power hardware timer (such as the TPL5010, MAX6369, or a dedicated low-pin-count microcontroller like an ATtiny) that counts down to zero unless periodically reset.
  2. High-Side Power Switch: A P-Channel MOSFET, solid-state relay, or dedicated load switch (e.g., TPS229xx series) placed on the positive power input rail between the primary power source and the SBC's power input.
  3. GPIO Heartbeat Line: A general-purpose input/output pin on the SBC configured to output a pulse at a fixed interval via a background service or daemon.
  4. Discharge Circuitry: Bleeder resistors or active discharge paths placed downstream of the switch to quickly drain onboard capacitors when power is severed.

Step-by-Step Operation

1. Normal Heartbeat Operation

Under normal operating conditions, a background software service on the SBC toggles the designated GPIO pin at a regular frequency (e.g., once every 10 seconds). The external watchdog IC detects these transitions on its input pin and continuously resets its internal countdown timer. As long as these pulses arrive before the timeout threshold, the watchdog IC maintains the high-side switch in a closed (conducting) state, allowing continuous power to the SBC.

2. Fault Detection

If the SBC experiences an operating system crash, power-rail glitch, or total software freeze, the background daemon stops executing. Consequently, the heartbeat pulses stop. The external watchdog timer continues counting down uninterrupted until its preset timeout window (e.g., 30 to 60 seconds) expires.

3. Power Interruption

Upon timing out, the watchdog IC changes the logic level driving the high-side switch's gate or enable pin.

  • For a P-Channel MOSFET, the gate voltage is pulled high (to match the source voltage), turning the transistor off.
  • The switch opens, disconnecting the SBC from the main DC input.
  • Bleeder resistors immediately drain residual voltage stored in the SBC’s decoupling and filtering capacitors to ensure the rail reaches true 0V.

4. Cold Reboot

The watchdog circuit holds the power switch open for a defined duration (typically 500 milliseconds to several seconds). Once this reset duration passes, the watchdog IC drives the switch active again, reapplying power to the SBC. The SBC detects fresh power on its input rail and initiates a full cold boot sequence.

Critical Design Considerations

  • Independent Power Supply: The external watchdog timer IC and gate-drive circuitry must receive power directly from the upstream power source, bypass-connected before the switching MOSFET.
  • Boot Delay Provision: The watchdog must feature an extended startup window (delay-on-boot) to prevent it from cycling power prematurely while the operating system initializes before starting the heartbeat daemon.
  • Current Handling: The switching element must be rated for the peak inrush current of the SBC to prevent thermal failure or voltage drop across the switch during normal operation.
