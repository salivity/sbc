How PCIe Lanes Impact SBC Expansion Capabilities

Peripheral Component Interconnect Express (PCIe) lane availability is one of the most critical factors determining the performance, versatility, and hardware scalability of a single board computer (SBC). This article examines how the number and generation of PCIe lanes dictate the types of peripherals an SBC can support—ranging from high-speed NVMe storage and multi-gigabit networking to dedicated AI accelerators—and explores how bandwidth allocation constraints shape real-world hardware integration.

Understanding PCIe Lanes and Bandwidth

A PCIe lane consists of two pairs of wires: one for transmitting data and one for receiving. Configurations are denoted as x1, x2, x4, x8, or x16, indicating the number of physical or logical lanes allocated to a single connection.

Bandwidth scales linearly with both the number of lanes and the PCIe generation:

  • PCIe 2.0: ~500 MB/s per lane
  • PCIe 3.0: ~985 MB/s per lane
  • PCIe 4.0: ~1.97 GB/s per lane

Because System-on-Chip (SoC) architectures used in SBCs have limited physical pins and internal controllers, the total pool of PCIe lanes is typically small—often between 1 and 4 lanes.

Storage Performance and Form Factors

The most common use of PCIe on modern SBCs is connecting solid-state storage via M.2 slots:

  • x1 Lane Allocation: Commonly found on budget or compact SBCs (such as via a HAT or small M.2 2230/2280 breakout). An M.2 NVMe drive running on a single PCIe 2.0 or 3.0 lane will operate at reduced sequential read/write speeds, though random access latency remains significantly better than eMMC or microSD cards.
  • x2 or x4 Lane Allocation: Higher-tier SBCs provide x2 or x4 interfaces, allowing standard NVMe SSDs to operate at or near their rated speeds. This enables SBCs to function efficiently as Network Attached Storage (NAS) units, database servers, or fast media capture nodes.

Specialized Peripherals and Edge AI

As edge computing advances, PCIe lanes are increasingly allocated to hardware beyond storage:

  • AI Acceleration Modules: Neural processing units (NPUs) like Google Coral, Hailo-8, or Intel Movidius cards in M.2 or Mini PCIe form factors require steady bandwidth. A bottlenecked x1 connection can throttle real-time inferencing pipelines handling high-resolution video streams.
  • Multi-Gigabit Networking: Adding 2.5GbE, 10GbE, or Wi-Fi 6/7/7E adapters requires dedicated PCIe bandwidth. A 10GbE network card requires at least a PCIe 3.0 x2 or PCIe 2.0 x4 link to prevent packet drops and saturation limits.
  • External GPUs and Video Capture: While rare, some SBCs expose open-ended PCIe slots or allow external GPU (eGPU) connectivity. An x4 link offers sufficient throughput for compute workloads (like GPGPU processing), whereas an x1 link severely limits data transfer between system memory and the GPU.

Multiplexing and PCIe Switches

When an SBC SoC features only one or two native PCIe lanes, manufacturers often use PCIe packet switches to expand peripheral options.

  • How It Works: A 1-to-3 packet switch takes a single upstream PCIe lane and splits it into multiple downstream lanes to drive an onboard Ethernet controller, a Wi-Fi module, and an M.2 slot simultaneously.
  • The Trade-Off: While multiplexing increases the total number of connected physical devices, they must dynamically share the bandwidth of the single upstream lane. Simultaneous high-load activity (such as writing to an NVMe drive while saturating a gigabit network) can lead to contention and reduced throughput across all shared interfaces.

Practical Implications for System Designers

When selecting an SBC for an expansion-heavy project:

  1. Count Total Usable Lanes: Verify whether exposed M.2 or expansion connectors provide native lanes or are routed through a shared switch.
  2. Match Peripherals to Lane Generations: A PCIe 4.0 x1 slot offers roughly the same raw bandwidth as a PCIe 3.0 x2 slot, but peripheral hardware must match the generation supported by the SoC to achieve peak throughput.
  3. Prioritize Dedicated vs. Shared Routing: For high-throughput tasks like continuous camera recording or fast local backups, choose boards that route unshared lanes directly to the primary expansion slots.
