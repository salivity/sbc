Multi-Layer PCB Power Routing Guide for SBCs

Routing power on a multi-layer printed circuit board (PCB) for a Single Board Computer (SBC) requires a balance between delivering clean power, minimizing electromagnetic interference (EMI), managing heat, and maintaining signal integrity. Modern SBCs incorporate high-performance System-on-Chips (SoCs), memory, and diverse peripherals that operate at low voltages and high transient currents. This guide details the essential technical considerations for power routing on a multi-layer SBC PCB, including stack-up architecture, impedance control, trace geometry, and decoupling strategies.

Layer Stack-Up and Plane Allocation

The foundation of low-noise power delivery is a well-designed layer stack-up. For an SBC, which typically utilizes 4 to 8 layers or more:

  • Continuous Ground Planes: Power planes or high-current traces should always be routed adjacent to a solid reference ground plane. This configuration minimizes the loop area of current return paths, directly reducing radiated EMI and parasitic inductance.
  • Planar Capacitance: Placing power planes and ground planes on tightly spaced adjacent dielectric layers creates inter-plane capacitance. This intrinsic capacitance helps filter ultra-high-frequency noise that discrete decoupling capacitors cannot mitigate effectively.
  • Symmetrical Stack-Up: Ensure the stack-up remains balanced across the board's core to prevent mechanical warping during the reflow soldering process.

Current Capacity and IR Drop Management

Modern SBC processors often draw heavy transient currents at voltages near or below 1.0V. Under these conditions, even minor DC resistance can cause unacceptable voltage drops (IR drops):

  • Trace Width and Copper Pours: Use wide copper polygons (power planes or islands) rather than thin tracks for major power rails such as core, GPU, and DDR memory supplies. Trace widths should be sized according to IPC-2152 standards based on target current and allowable temperature rise.
  • Voltage Margin Tolerances: Keep power paths as short and wide as possible between the Power Management Integrated Circuit (PMIC) and the load to ensure power rails remain within strict operational tolerances (typically ±3% to ±5%).

Decoupling Capacitor Placement and Routing

Decoupling networks provide instantaneous current to ICs during high-frequency switching events:

  • Proximity to Load: Place the smallest value, highest-frequency capacitors (e.g., 0.1 µF or smaller in 0201/0402 packages) as close as physically possible to the power pins of the SoC and memory chips. Larger bulk capacitors can be placed slightly further away.
  • Minimizing Parasitic Inductance: Connect capacitor pads directly to ground and power vias using short, wide traces. Avoid routing traces from the IC pin through a long track before reaching the capacitor; power must flow through the capacitor pads or via connections directly adjacent to them.
  • Via Placement: Place decoupling vias side-by-side rather than in-line to reduce mutual inductance.

Via Transitions and Parasitic Inductance

Vias introduce parasitic inductance and resistance that can degrade the Power Delivery Network (PDN):

  • Multiple Vias: Always use multiple parallel vias when transitioning high-current power rails between layers. Paralleling vias divides the equivalent series resistance (ESR) and equivalent series inductance (ESL).
  • Via-in-Pad: For fine-pitch Ball Grid Arrays (BGAs) common on SBC processors, utilize via-in-pad technology filled and capped with planar copper to eliminate escape routing inductance.

Noise Isolation and Domain Partitioning

SBCs combine high-noise digital processing with noise-sensitive analog components, such as audio codecs, ADCs, and RF interfaces:

  • Split Power Islands: Divide the power distribution into distinct regional islands (e.g., Core, I/O, DDR, Analog) rather than sharing a single noisy rail across the entire board.
  • Star Topology: Distribute power from the PMIC using a star topology where individual rails branch out from a central regulation point, preventing noise from digital switching circuits from coupling into sensitive analog sections.
  • Ferrite Beads and Filtering: Insert ferrite beads or low-pass LC filters on power feeds entering sensitive domains, placing local decoupling capacitors immediately after the filter.

Thermal Management

Power distribution components, including PMICs, linear regulators, and high-current traces, generate localized heat:

  • Thermal Vias: Place arrays of thermal vias directly beneath power IC exposed pads to transfer heat downward into internal and bottom-layer ground planes.
  • Copper Weight: Consider using 2 oz (70 µm) copper on outer or dedicated power layers to enhance heat dissipation and reduce current resistance across the board.
