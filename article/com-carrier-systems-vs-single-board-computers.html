COM Carrier Systems vs Single Board Computers

When selecting embedded hardware for industrial, commercial, or IoT applications, the two primary architectural approaches are Computer-on-Module (COM) carrier systems and standalone Single Board Computers (SBCs). While both solutions provide full processing capability and interface connectivity, they differ fundamentally in their modularity, scalability, development costs, and lifecycle management. This guide outlines the key differences to help you determine the optimal platform for your project.

Architecture and Physical Design

A Single Board Computer (SBC) integrates the entire computing system onto a single printed circuit board (PCB). The microprocessor, RAM, storage, power circuitry, and standard input/output (I/O) ports (such as USB, Ethernet, and HDMI) are permanently fixed in place.

A Computer-on-Module (COM) system splits this architecture into two distinct components:

  1. The Module (or System-on-Module): Contains the core computing components, including the processor, memory, core power management, and graphics chipset.
  2. The Carrier Board (Baseboard): Houses the application-specific connectors, power supplies, custom I/O interfaces, and physical mounting hardware.

The module connects to the carrier board via standardized, high-density board-to-board connectors.

Customization and Form Factor Flexibility

  • SBCs: Designed primarily as standard, off-the-shelf solutions. Their form factor, port placement, and connector selection are fixed. Customizing an SBC generally requires developing an entirely new board from scratch or adding bulky expansion shields.
  • COM Systems: Offer near-total design flexibility. Developers can design a custom carrier board tailored exactly to the space constraints and peripheral requirements of the end product, without needing to route complex, high-speed memory and processor buses.

Scalability and Upgrade Paths

  • SBCs: Upgrading performance typically requires replacing the entire board. If the updated SBC changes its layout or connector positions, the outer enclosure and internal cabling must also be redesigned.
  • COM Systems: Built around standard form-factor specifications (such as COM Express, SMARC, or Qseven). If higher processing power is required later, the existing module can be swapped for a higher-performance module using the same pinout, leaving the carrier board and enclosure untouched.

Development Cost and Time to Market

  • SBCs: Offer the fastest time-to-market and lowest initial upfront engineering costs for low-to-medium production volumes. You purchase ready-to-run hardware with pre-existing software support.
  • COM Systems: Require an initial investment in carrier board design and validation. However, because the complex computing core is already engineered and certified on the module, carrier board development is significantly faster and less expensive than building a fully custom SBC.

Obsolescence and Lifecycle Management

  • SBCs: Vulnerable to component obsolescence. If a core chip on the SBC reaches End-of-Life (EOL), the entire system often requires a major redesign.
  • COM Systems: Insulate products from component EOL cycles. When a module's processor is discontinued, the manufacturer typically provides a pin-compatible next-generation module, allowing products to remain in production for decades with minimal redesign effort.

Summary Comparison

Feature Single Board Computer (SBC) COM + Carrier System
Architecture Monolithic (All-in-one) Two-piece (Module + Carrier)
Custom I/O Fixed/Limited Fully customizable
Upgradability Low (Full replacement) High (Module swap)
Design Complexity None (Off-the-shelf) Moderate (Baseboard design only)
Initial Cost Low Moderate (Carrier NRE)
