DAC Power Supply Noise on Single Board Computers

High-resolution Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs) are inherently vulnerable to power supply noise, which can severely degrade dynamic range, Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), and Total Harmonic Distortion (THD). On compact Single Board Computers (SBCs), high-density digital processors and switching regulators operate in close proximity to sensitive mixed-signal components. This article outlines the primary mechanisms through which power supply noise couples into high-resolution DAC architectures on SBC platforms.

Switch-Mode Power Supply (SMPS) Ripple and Harmonics

SBCs rely heavily on switch-mode buck and boost regulators to efficiently step down supply voltages for multi-core processors. These regulators generate high-frequency switching ripple, along with fast-transient switching spikes across broad frequency spectra. Because high-resolution DACs (16-bit, 24-bit, or 32-bit) feature Power Supply Rejection Ratios (PSRR) that decline sharply as frequency increases, high-frequency SMPS ripple easily bypasses internal rejection stages and modulates directly onto the analog output signal.

Reference Voltage (VREF) Contamination

A DAC's output voltage is a direct multiplication of its digital input code and its reference voltage (\(V_{REF}\)). Consequently, the power supply rejection of the reference voltage pin is effectively zero decibels at all frequencies. When the reference rail is derived from a noisy system supply, shared with digital logic, or inadequately isolated from switching transients, any voltage fluctuation on \(V_{REF}\) modulates the output directly, producing sideband spurs and phase noise.

Common-Impedance Coupling via Ground Planes

SBCs feature dense layouts with high-speed digital buses, DDR memory, and high-frequency clock signals. When analog DAC ground returns share a path or plane with noisy digital return currents, the finite impedance and inductance of the copper plane cause common-impedance coupling. This mechanism allows digital transient currents (\(dI/dt\)) to generate localized ground voltage fluctuations (\(V = L \cdot dI/dt\)), lifting the DAC’s local ground and appearing directly as differential noise across the analog output.

Inadequate Decoupling and Trace Parasitics

Improper component placement and routing introduce parasitic resistance and trace inductance between bypass capacitors and the DAC pins. High-frequency transient currents cannot be effectively shunted to ground if the decoupling capacitors have long via connections, narrow traces, or excessive loop areas. This leads to resonant ringing on the DAC supply rails, elevating the overall noise floor and creating spectral spurs.

Capacitive and Inductive Crosstalk from Digital Lines

Dense SBC routing often forces high-speed digital traces (such as SPI, I2S, and clock lines) to run parallel to, or cross directly beneath, sensitive analog power and reference traces. High edge-rate digital signals (\(dV/dt\)) capacitively couple noise into adjacent high-impedance power pins, while rapid magnetic flux changes from switching inductors and high-current traces inductively couple into sensitive analog loops.
