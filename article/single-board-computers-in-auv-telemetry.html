Single Board Computers in AUV Telemetry

Single board computers (SBCs) serve as the primary computational backbone for managing telemetry in modern autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs). By acting as the central hub between onboard sensors, internal health diagnostics, and multi-modal communication systems, SBCs process, compress, and route vital operational data in real time. This article explores how SBCs interface with subsea instrumentation, optimize critical bandwidth-constrained communications, monitor vehicle diagnostics, and balance computing power with strict energy limitations in underwater environments.

Sensor Data Aggregation and Protocol Translation

Modern AUVs carry diverse payloads, including Doppler Velocity Logs (DVLs), Inertial Navigation Systems (INS), conductivity-temperature-depth (CTD) sensors, and multibeam sonars. These instruments communicate using various legacy and modern interfaces such as RS-232, RS-485, I2C, SPI, CAN bus, and Ethernet.

SBCs bridge these disparate protocols. Running embedded operating systems like Linux or real-time operating systems (RTOS), the SBC collects real-time outputs from all onboard instruments, timestamps them with microsecond precision, and synchronizes the streams into a unified telemetry log.

Edge Processing and Telemetry Compression

Underwater acoustic communication channels suffer from extreme physical limitations, offering data rates typically ranging between tens of bits per second to a few kilobits per second. Telemetry streams cannot be transmitted raw over acoustic modems.

SBCs execute edge-computing algorithms to prepare telemetry for transmission:

  • Filtering and Downsampling: Extracting high-priority state variables—such as depth, heading, battery state-of-charge, and critical system faults—while discarding high-frequency noise.
  • Data Compression: Applying specialized lossless or lossy compression algorithms to minimize packet sizes.
  • Priority Queuing: Structuring dynamic packet queues so safety-critical alerts override routine scientific metadata when bandwidth drops.

Multi-Modal Communication Management

An AUV encounters different operational environments throughout its mission profile, requiring dynamic telemetry routing across multiple transceivers:

  1. Submerged Mode: The SBC routes heavily compressed, critical status packets through acoustic modems to surface vessels or topside buoys.
  2. Surfaced Mode (Nearshore): When the vehicle surfaces near a base or vessel, the SBC switches the telemetry stream to high-speed Wi-Fi or line-of-sight RF radios to upload full mission data dumps.
  3. Surfaced Mode (Offshore): In open-ocean deployments, the SBC establishes a connection via satellite transceivers (such as Iridium) to transmit vehicle coordinates, mission progress, and health metrics to remote ground stations.

The SBC manages this switching logic autonomously based on GPS acquisition, depth sensor thresholds, and network availability.

System Health Monitoring and Fault Handling

Beyond external communications, SBCs manage internal system telemetry to safeguard the platform:

  • Monitoring internal hull pressure, relative humidity, and leak-detection circuits.
  • Tracking power distribution, individual cell voltages, and thermal states across battery modules.
  • Executing fail-safe routines (e.g., aborting missions, dropping emergency drop-weights, or triggering ascent maneuvers) if internal telemetry breaches predefined safety thresholds.

Energy Efficiency and Form Factor Advantages

AUVs operate under rigid space and power constraints inside sealed pressure vessels. Traditional multi-board industrial computers generate excessive heat and drain large amounts of battery capacity, while simple microcontrollers lack the processing power and OS-level network stacks required for complex data management.

SBCs provide an optimal balance. With small form factors (such as COM Express, PC/104, or Raspberry Pi Compute Modules) and low thermal design power (TDP), they deliver the multi-core processing, large memory bandwidth, and extensive I/O capacity required to manage modern subsea telemetry pipelines without compromising mission endurance.
