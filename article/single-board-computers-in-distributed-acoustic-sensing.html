Single Board Computers in Distributed Acoustic Sensing

Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS) utilizes standard fiber-optic cables as continuous arrays of acoustic and vibration sensors, generating massive volumes of high-frequency data. To manage these computational and logistical demands, researchers increasingly integrate Single Board Computers (SBCs) into DAS architectures. This article explores how SBCs function as edge-computing nodes, timing synchronization controllers, and remote telemetry hubs, allowing research teams to reduce bandwidth, deploy localized machine learning models, and build cost-effective, scalable monitoring networks.

Edge Data Pre-Processing and Filtering

Raw DAS interrogator units generate immense data streams, frequently reaching hundreds of megabytes per second per fiber channel. Transmitting this raw telemetry to central servers or cloud repositories is often impractical, especially in remote field deployments. Researchers utilize SBCs—such as Raspberry Pi, BeagleBone, or custom ARM-based modules—to perform localized pre-processing directly at the interrogator site.

SBCs run real-time digital signal processing (DSP) pipelines, including:

  • Decimation and Downsampling: Lowering sampling rates for low-frequency phenomena such as microseismic monitoring.
  • Spatial and Temporal Bandpass Filtering: Eliminating environmental noise (e.g., wind or traffic) to isolate specific acoustic frequencies.
  • Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT): Converting time-domain strain measurements into frequency-domain spectra for immediate baseline analysis.

Real-Time Event Detection and Edge AI

Where low latency is critical, researchers deploy SBCs equipped with integrated graphics processing units (GPUs) or neural processing units (NPUs), such as NVIDIA Jetson or Google Coral development boards. These edge AI platforms run lightweight convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to detect acoustic anomalies instantaneously.

Applications include:

  • Pipeline and Infrastructure Monitoring: Detecting structural deformation, fluid leaks, or unauthorized third-party interference.
  • Border and Perimeter Security: Distinguishing between human footsteps, vehicles, and animal movements along monitored perimeters.
  • Geophysical Hazard Warnings: Identifying early P-wave arrivals during seismic events to trigger automated shutoff systems.

By classifying events on the edge, the system only needs to transmit alert flags and small snippets of acoustic data, vastly conserving network bandwidth.

Precise Time-Stamping and Network Synchronization

DAS systems require microsecond-level synchronization across large geographic distances to correlate acoustic signals recorded by different interrogators or external geophones. Researchers integrate GPS/GNSS receiver expansion boards (HATs) with SBCs to act as localized time servers.

Using protocols such as the Precision Time Protocol (PTP / IEEE 1588) or Network Time Protocol (NTP), the SBC synchronizes the internal clock of the DAS interrogator. This synchronization ensures that spatial-temporal phase shifts across kilometers of fiber optic cable are precisely mapped for accurate source localization and beamforming.

Telemetry, Remote Management, and Environmental Control

Field-deployed DAS systems are often located in harsh or inaccessible environments, such as offshore platforms, geothermal fields, and boreholes. SBCs serve as low-power management microcontrollers that supervise the physical deployment.

Researchers program SBCs to:

  • Monitor Environmental Conditions: Interface with I2C and SPI sensors to track temperature, humidity, and power consumption within interrogator enclosures.
  • Manage Power States: Trigger automated sleep and wake cycles, or switch to backup solar/battery power during grid outages.
  • Handle Cellular and Satellite Uplinks: Manage secure VPN tunnels and cellular modems (LTE/5G) to route high-priority alerts to offsite researchers.

Advantages in Field Research

Integrating single board computers into DAS networks provides significant research advantages:

  • Cost Efficiency: SBCs offer enterprise-grade computing capabilities at a fraction of the cost of industrial rack-mount servers.
  • Low Power Consumption: Most SBCs operate within a 5W to 15W power envelope, making them ideal for solar- and battery-powered field stations.
  • Flexibility and Open-Source Integration: Researchers can deploy custom Python, C++, or Rust algorithms on standard Linux distributions without proprietary vendor lock-in.
