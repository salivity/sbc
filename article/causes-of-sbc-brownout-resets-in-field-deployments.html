Causes of SBC Brownout Resets in Field Deployments

A brownout reset occurs when the input voltage to a Single Board Computer (SBC) drops below the minimum operating threshold, triggering an unexpected system reboot or freeze. In field deployments, these power dips often lead to intermittent failures, data corruption, and operational downtime. Understanding the primary causes of brownout resets—ranging from power supply limitations to environmental stresses—is critical for designing resilient, remote SBC installations.

Inadequate Power Supply Sizing

SBCs exhibit dynamic power profiles. While an SBC might idle at a low current, operations such as CPU/GPU bursts, storage writes, or wireless transmissions (LTE, Wi-Fi, LoRa) cause rapid current spikes. If a power supply unit (PSU) is rated only for the average operating current rather than peak transient draw, these sudden surges collapse the voltage rail momentarily, triggering the SBC's internal voltage supervisory circuit to reset the board.

Cable Length and Voltage Drop (\(I \times R\) Losses)

Field installations often require placing the SBC away from the main power source. Thin gauge wiring (high AWG) combined with long cable runs introduces significant electrical resistance. When the SBC draws sudden peak currents, the resistance in the wire causes a proportional voltage drop across the line (\(V = I \times R\)). Even if the power source maintains an exact 5V or 12V output, the voltage arriving at the SBC terminals can dip below the brownout threshold.

Peripheral Inrush Current

Connecting external peripherals directly to the SBC's onboard headers or USB ports can destabilize the primary power rail. Devices such as high-draw USB modems, external mechanical hard drives, relays, and motor drivers demand high inrush current upon activation. Without dedicated power regulation, soft-start circuits, or adequate local decoupling capacitors, this sudden demand pulls down the SBC's main supply rail.

Temperature-Induced Component Degradation

Field-deployed systems operate across wide ambient temperature ranges. Extreme cold increases the internal resistance (ESR) of electrolytic capacitors in power supplies, reducing their ability to buffer transient loads. Conversely, high temperatures reduce the power output efficiency of voltage regulators and degrade battery chemistries. Both extremes lower the power delivery capacity of the system, making voltage drops more frequent.

Unstable Primary Power and Battery Sag

Off-grid installations reliant on solar panels, wind generators, or batteries frequently face power fluctuations. As batteries age, their internal resistance rises, causing rapid voltage sags under load. Solar systems without adequate battery buffering or poorly regulated DC-DC converters can drop input voltages during cloud cover or sudden load changes, dropping supply rails below safe operating limits.

Inductive Loads and Industrial Noise

In industrial field settings, SBCs often operate near inductive loads like solenoids, contactors, or heavy motors. The switching of these loads induces high-energy voltage spikes and ground bounce on shared electrical lines. If the SBC lacks proper galvanic isolation, transient voltage suppressors (TVS), or line filtering, this electrical noise can falsely trigger the microcontroller's brownout detection circuitry.
