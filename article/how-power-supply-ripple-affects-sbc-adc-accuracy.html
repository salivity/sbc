How Power Supply Ripple Affects SBC ADC Accuracy

Power supply ripple voltage introduces electrical noise into the internal Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) of a single-board computer (SBC), leading to reduced measurement precision, erratic sensor readings, and compromised data integrity. This article explains the technical mechanisms through which power rail fluctuations degrade ADC performance—focusing on reference voltage shifting, Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR) limitations, and timing jitter—and outlines key mitigation methods to preserve analog accuracy.

Voltage Reference Instability

Most integrated ADCs on SBCs derive their conversion reference voltage directly from the system’s primary power rail (typically 3.3V or 5V) rather than a dedicated, precision reference source. Because an ADC quantizes an input voltage by comparing it to this reference voltage (\(V_{REF}\)), any ripple or drift on the power line causes \(V_{REF}\) to fluctuate. Consequently, the value of the least significant bit (LSB) changes dynamically, causing identical analog input levels to produce varying digital outputs depending on where in the ripple phase the sample is taken.

Limitations of Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR)

The internal circuitry of an ADC—including operational amplifiers, analog multiplexers, and sample-and-hold capacitors—has a finite Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR). While PSRR is often adequate at very low frequencies, it degrades significantly at higher switching frequencies typically produced by onboard switch-mode power supplies (often between 100 kHz and several MHz). High-frequency ripple easily bypasses internal regulation and couples directly into the sampling path, manifesting as unwanted periodic noise superimposed onto the measured signal.

Reduction in ENOB, SNR, and Linearity

When ripple voltage infiltrates the conversion process, it directly degrades key ADC performance metrics:

  • Effective Number of Bits (ENOB): Ripple adds noise floor energy, artificially limiting a nominal 10-bit or 12-bit ADC to significantly lower true resolution.
  • Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and SINAD: Ripple appears as discrete spurs or broadband noise in the frequency domain, worsening harmonic distortion and dynamic range.
  • Integral and Differential Non-Linearity (INL/DNL): Sudden voltage drops during sampling cycles can cause missing codes or uneven step widths across the conversion range.

Clock Jitter and Sampling Errors

Power rail fluctuations can also introduce phase noise and jitter into the internal system clocks and oscillators that drive the ADC’s sampling intervals. Inconsistent sampling times (\(t\)) distort dynamic signal acquisition, particularly for rapidly changing analog inputs, converting time-domain jitter into amplitude error.

Methods to Mitigate Ripple Effects

To minimize the impact of power ripple on internal SBC ADCs:

  • Use an External Precision Voltage Reference: Decouple the ADC reference from the main power rail by utilizing a dedicated, low-noise bandgap reference IC.
  • Add Low-Dropout (LDO) Regulators and Passive Filtering: Place an ultra-low-noise LDO, ferrite beads, and high-quality ceramic decoupling capacitors as close to the analog supply pins as possible.
  • Employ Oversampling and Digital Filtering: Implementing software-based moving average filters or decimation can cancel out periodic high-frequency ripple, provided the ripple frequency does not alias into the target bandwidth.
